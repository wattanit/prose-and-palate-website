**Note: Grayscale Fine Art & Bar permanently closed in March 2026. This review is preserved as a record of a genuinely original gallery-and-cocktail-bar hybrid that, for a while, was well worth the trip to Charoen Nakhon.**

<p class="dropcap">Tucked into a quiet backstreet near ICONSIAM, on the Charoen Nakhon side of the river, Grayscale Fine Art & Bar commits to its name in a way most themed bars don't bother to. This isn't a cocktail bar with a few pieces of art hung up for atmosphere. It's a working photography gallery, framed black-and-white prints lining a narrow white corridor, that happens to open into a bar at the end of it. Walk in past the gate-like glass storefront, and the space unfolds gradually: gallery wall, then banquette seating, then the bar itself.</p>

<Section heading="A Gallery You Can Drink In" id="sec-1">

<Row>
<Figure id="lb-gf-3" src="/images/posts/bars/2025/grayscale-fineart-bar/4.jpg" alt="The atmosphere" caption="Minimalist, black and white, very bright." />

The design commits fully to its own premise. Everything is rendered in white, grey, and the occasional deep charcoal—an actual grayscale, applied to the walls, the banquettes, the recessed lighting, even the round marble side tables. The black-and-white photography isn't wallpaper; it's genuinely worth pausing in front of on the walk to your seat. It is, without much competition, one of the most photogenic rooms in this entire project's back catalogue—the kind of place where every angle looks considered, because it was.
</Row>

<Row variant="alt">
That discipline pays off in atmosphere more than anywhere else. The bar counter is compact, the seating intimate, and the whole thing feels like an extension of the gallery rather than a separate business sharing the lease. Whether that's a clever piece of branding or genuine curatorial intent, it works.

<Figure id="lb-gf-4" src="/images/posts/bars/2025/grayscale-fineart-bar/5.jpg" alt="The bar" caption="Compact and clean bar, with just 3 stools." />
</Row>

<Row variant="alt">
Above the bar, the collection matches the place's quiet confidence—shelves of spirits interspersed with cocktail books and stacks of records, the kind of back-bar that reads as a working library rather than a display case.

<Section heading="The Infusion Specialist" id="sec-2">

Where Grayscale gets more interesting is in the glass. The signature menu is built entirely around infused base spirits—sweet potato, black sesame, agarwood—each one doing serious work inside a deceptively simple three-or-four-ingredient build. It's a smart way to generate real variety, and the house infusions themselves are the strongest thing on the menu: distinctive, well-judged, and clearly the product of a lot of quiet experimentation behind the scenes.

<Row>
<Figure id="lb-gf-6" src="/images/posts/bars/2025/grayscale-fineart-bar/11.jpg" alt="Drink Acrylic Paint" caption="Acrylic Paint" />

**Acrylic Paint** pairs white rum and dry vermouth with potato syrup and a float of aged rum, finished with aromatic bitters and a garnish of sweet-and-purple-potato chips balanced across the ice. Before you even sip, there's a faint sweet-potato aroma sitting on top of the glass. The drink itself is round and smooth, gently sweet, with none of the sharper edges you'd expect from something built on two rums and a fortified wine. It reads as more approachable than its spirit-forward ingredient list suggests.
</Row>

<Row variant="alt">
**Monochrome** takes fig-infused bourbon and stacks it against dry sherry, Cynar, and a neri goma (Japanese black sesame paste) Sichuan syrup—structurally somewhere between an Old Fashioned and a Boulevardier, but darker and stranger. The nose is unmistakably black sesame, an aroma you don't expect to find hovering over a bourbon drink, and it carries through nicely into a strong, well-composed first sip. It's the most interesting build on the menu, and one of the few places where the recipe complexity actually shows up in the glass.

<Figure id="lb-gf-7" src="/images/posts/bars/2025/grayscale-fineart-bar/12.jpg" alt="Drink Monochrome" caption="Monochrome" />
</Row>

<Row>
<Figure id="lb-gf-8" src="/images/posts/bars/2025/grayscale-fineart-bar/13.jpg" alt="Pencil Color" caption="Pencil Color" />

**Pencil Color** was the standout of the visit. Built on aged eagle wood (agarwood) gin instead of any whiskey, with maple shrub, vanilla, lemon, egg white, and peach bitters, it drinks like a smoky whiskey sour despite containing no whiskey at all. The agarwood does exactly the job you'd want a peated scotch to do—lending smoke and depth—while the maple and vanilla round out the sweetness and the egg white keeps the texture smooth. Garnished with a dried flower and flecks of peach bitters across the foam, it's as good to look at as it is to drink, and easily the most well-resolved cocktail of the three.
</Row>

</Section>

<Section heading="An Infusion Program With Real Teeth" id="sec-3">

The easy assumption about an infusion-driven menu is that it's a shortcut—let the jar do the work, keep the build simple, and call it a day. Grayscale mostly disproves that. The infusions themselves show real technical judgment: the sweet potato syrup is balanced rather than cloying, the black sesame Sichuan syrup is aromatic without overwhelming the bourbon underneath it, and the agarwood gin threads a genuinely difficult needle between smoke and sweetness. It isn't flawless—nothing here reaches the razor-sharp precision of a bar built entirely around technique—but the bartenders clearly know what they're doing with their own ingredients, and it shows in how well-resolved each drink is by the time it reaches the table.

</Section>

<Section heading="Final Thoughts" id="sec-4" variant="prose">

Grayscale earned its visit for the room, but it kept you there for the drinks. Sweet potato, black sesame, and agarwood are not combinations you stumble into by accident, and turning each of them into a genuinely drinkable, well-balanced cocktail took real skill, not just a good idea. Paired with a gallery space this considered, it made for one of the more original and satisfying nights out in Bangkok. Honestly, it's a real loss that it's now gone.

</Section>
